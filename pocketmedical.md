# Full report: Gamification in healthcare by patient group (Sep 2026)


Games and gamification help some patients, but mostly with **narrow, specific outcomes**: the skill the game trains, how much of an already-proven activity (exercise, rehab) people actually do, or how much pain or fear they feel in the moment. They rarely change the wider illness. The clearest wins are **amblyopia** ("lazy eye"), where cleared devices improve eyesight about as much as patching ([FDA DEN210005](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/reviews/DEN210005.pdf); [Ophthalmology Times](https://www.ophthalmologytimes.com/view/novasight-publishes-curesight-s-pivotal-study-of-1-year-follow-up-outcomes)); **children's ADHD**, where EndeavorRx improves scores on a computer attention test but not parent-rated symptoms ([FDA DEN200026](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/reviews/DEN200026.pdf)); **stroke**, where game-based rehab helps arm function when it is added on top of normal therapy ([Cochrane](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD008349_virtual-reality-stroke-rehabilitation)); **older adults**, where exercise games cut falls ([JMIR Aging 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13427072/)); **smokers**, where gamified quit programmes nearly double short-term quitting ([Ho et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12199787/)); and **children having needles**, where game and VR distraction reduces pain ([PMC11417701](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11417701/)). Evidence is thin or missing for pregnancy (outside VR in labour), caregivers, ICU patients, people with sensory impairment, and low-literacy or rural users in India. The pattern that matters most for Pocket Plays is this: **brain-training games get better at the trained task, but that gain rarely spreads to daily life** ([Westwood et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36977764/); [FTC v. Lumos Labs](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2016/01/lumosity-pay-2-million-settle-ftc-deceptive-advertising-charges-its-brain-training-program)). A free wellness game in the US, EU and India stays outside medical-device rules only if it never names a disease it treats, prevents, diagnoses or manages ([FDA wellness guidance via Covington](https://www.cov.com/en/news-and-insights/insights/2026/01/fda-issues-revised-guidance-on-general-wellness-products); [MDCG 2019-11](https://health.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2020-09/md_mdcg_2019_11_guidance_en_0.pdf); [Emergo on CDSCO](https://www.emergobyul.com/news/india-cdsco-finalizes-guidance-medical-device-software)). That fits the existing decision to call Pocket Plays "focus skills" practice and never treatment. None of the 46 games is cleared or prescribed. Four of them (Bullseye, Opposite, Rule Switch, Clash) have a real safety problem with screen flashes, and that should be fixed before any outreach to patient groups ([WCAG 2.3.1](https://w3c.github.io/wcag21/understanding/three-flashes-or-below-threshold.html)).

A note on how to read this report. The evidence ratings below use five levels. **Strong** means large, consistent trials or meta-analyses. **Moderate** means a meta-analysis with mixed results, or one or two good trials. **Low** means small trials or pilots. **Very low** means feasibility or design work only. **None** means no usable evidence was found. Some numbers in the research notes were remembered from abstracts rather than re-checked; they are marked **(unverified)** below, and a few were left out entirely.

## Children: real results for lazy eye and ADHD tests, weaker elsewhere

**ADHD** is where most people's picture of a "medical video game" comes from. EndeavorRx won FDA De Novo authorisation in 2020 after the STARS-ADHD trial: 348 children aged 8–12, randomised and double-blind ([FDA DEN200026](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/reviews/DEN200026.pdf)). Its main measure, a computerised attention test (TOVA), improved **0.93 points versus 0.03** for the control game. But **seven parent and clinician rating scales showed "no statistically meaningful difference"**. About 6.7% of children had mild side effects such as frustration or headache ([FDA DEN200026](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/reviews/DEN200026.pdf)). The label now covers ages 8–17. It is worded narrowly, "to improve attention function as measured by computer-based testing", at about 25 minutes a day, 5 days a week, and only as part of a wider treatment plan ([FDA K231337](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/pdf23/K231337.pdf); [Business Wire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20231218891755/en/EndeavorRx-Worlds-First-and-Only-Prescription-Video-Game-Treatment-Secures-FDA-Label-Expansion-for-Pediatric-ADHD-Patients-Aged-13-17)). The teen and adult over-the-counter versions were cleared on single-arm studies with no control group ([PMC12657867](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12657867/)). Akili has since been bought by Virtual Therapeutics ([MassDevice](https://www.massdevice.com/virtual-therapeutics-acquires-akili/)). The wider research agrees. A 2023 meta-analysis of 36 trials found that when outcomes were rated blind, brain training had **no effect on total ADHD symptoms, inhibition, reading or maths**. It did find gains in verbal working memory (0.38) and visual working memory (0.49), plus a small effect on inattention (0.17) that mostly showed up where the training happened ([Westwood et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36977764/)). Put plainly: children get better at memory and attention *tasks*, not at ADHD in daily life. That matches the broader finding that working-memory training transfers poorly ([Melby-Lervåg 2016](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1745691616635612)).

**Amblyopia** is the clearest case of a screen-based treatment changing a real clinical result. Luminopia shows children TV shows that have been altered so both eyes must work together (passive viewing, not a game). In a trial of 117 children aged 4–7 wearing glasses, the weak eye gained **1.81 lines on the eye chart versus 0.85** with glasses alone ([FDA DEN210005](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/reviews/DEN210005.pdf)). A later clearance extended use to ages 8–12 ([Healio 2025](https://www.healio.com/news/optometry/20250423/fda-clears-luminopia-for-amblyopia-in-children-aged-8-to-12-years-old)). CureSight, which uses eye-tracking, was **as good as eye patching** in 103 children; at one year some gain faded but about 2 lines remained ([Ophthalmology Times](https://www.ophthalmologytimes.com/view/novasight-publishes-curesight-s-pivotal-study-of-1-year-follow-up-outcomes); [Healio 2022](https://www.healio.com/news/optometry/20221024/novasight-announces-fda-clearance-of-curesight-digital-therapy-for-amblyopia)). These work because the screen directly trains the weak eye. An ordinary phone game does not do that.

For **autism**, a 2025 meta-analysis of 24 trials (1,801 children) found games improved social skills in children (g=0.81) but **not in teenagers** (g=0.16). They also helped attention (g=0.66). There was **no effect on language, anxiety or parent stress**, and most studies were not blinded ([Frontiers in Pediatrics 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/pediatrics/articles/10.3389/fped.2025.1498563/full)). For **dyslexia**, one Australian trial of 64 children found that 5 hours of an action game (Fruit Ninja) improved reading more than phonics lessons over two weeks, but there was no long-term follow-up ([Sci Rep 2021](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-021-98146-x)). For **cerebral palsy and DCD**, several meta-analyses report balance and motor gains from VR and active video games ([JMIR Serious Games 2022](https://games.jmir.org/2022/4/e38972); [PMC12607113](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12607113/)), though one earlier review questioned the effect ([PubMed 30311491](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30311491/)); these are movement games, not tapping games. For **dyscalculia and intellectual disability**, no trial results were verified; tools such as The Number Race exist ([PMC1523349](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1523349/)) but their trials were not checked for this report, so these groups are rated "none (verified)".

**Epilepsy** is a safety question, not a treatment one. No trial shows games treat childhood epilepsy. About **3% of people with epilepsy are photosensitive**, most often starting between ages 7 and 19, and flashing at **3 to 30 times per second** is the main trigger ([Epilepsy Society](https://epilepsysociety.org.uk/about-epilepsy/epileptic-seizures/seizure-triggers/photosensitive-epilepsy)). Web accessibility rules say content should not flash more than three times in any one second ([WCAG 2.3.1](https://w3c.github.io/wcag21/understanding/three-flashes-or-below-threshold.html)), and game guidelines say to avoid flicker and repeating patterns ([Game Accessibility Guidelines](https://gameaccessibilityguidelines.com/avoid-flickering-images-and-repetitive-patterns/)).

## Mental health and addiction: games mostly help people stick with therapy

In **youth depression**, a 2022 meta-analysis found a medium effect from gaming interventions in randomised trials (**g = −0.54**), but results varied a lot between studies (I² = 84%), and there was **no effect on anxiety** (g = −0.05) ([Townsend et al. 2022](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/bjpsych-open/article/effectiveness-of-gaming-interventions-for-depression-and-anxiety-in-young-people-systematic-review-and-metaanalysis/7206B6C2FCB40355582267D37A9B7231)). The best-known example, SPARX, is a fantasy game that teaches CBT to teenagers. In its trial it was "as effective as usual care" for mild-to-moderate depression ([PubMed 22773784](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22773784/)); the exact trial size and remission rates in the notes are **unverified**. The key point is that SPARX works because of its **therapy content**. One meta-analysis reportedly found that gamified depression apps did no better than the same apps without game features, which would mean game mechanics mainly boost engagement (**unverified**; [Six et al. 2021](https://mental.jmir.org/2021/11/e32199)). A separate study found gamifying a mental-health training task raised engagement and enjoyment, which is not a clinical result ([ScienceDirect 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0005791622000052)).

For **trauma**, playing about 20 minutes of Tetris within 6 hours of a car accident, after a brief memory reminder, reduced intrusive memories over the next week ([Iyadurai 2017](https://www.nature.com/articles/mp201723)). A 2026 review of four real-world studies (314 people) confirmed the week-one effect, but results at 1–6 months were "mixed and inconclusive", and one study found the effect reversed at 6 months ([Parkes 2026](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2589979126000430)). A six-lab replication found Tetris cut intrusions during play but **not over the following week** ([Collabra 2025](https://online.ucpress.edu/collabra/article/11/1/130791/207825/Evidence-That-Tetris-Reduces-Immediate-but-Not)). This is a timed clinical protocol, not a general benefit of block games.

For **schizophrenia**, computerised cognitive remediation, much of it drill-style software, has the largest evidence base of any group, with small benefits for thinking and daily functioning that are bigger when a therapist is involved ([Vita 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33877289/); [Wykes 2011](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/full/10.1176/appi.ajp.2010.10060855)). The specific effect sizes in the notes are **unverified**. For **insomnia, eating disorders, anxiety and postpartum depression**, the regulator-cleared products are CBT programmes, not games: Somryst, Sleepio, DaylightRx and MamaLift Plus in the US ([PMC12657867](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12657867/)), and CBT apps on Germany's DiGA list ([diga-verzeichnis.de](https://www.diga-verzeichnis.de/en/digas/psyche)). Rejoyn, a US-cleared depression app, uses a memory task with emotional faces that is game-like, but it beat the sham only in one type of analysis ([PMC12657867](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12657867/)).

**Addiction** has the best case for game mechanics themselves. A 2025 meta-analysis of 15 trials (5,075 people) found gamified smoking-cessation programmes raised short-term quitting (**RR 1.91**) and long-term quitting (**RR 1.37**), with the effect fading over time; competition, rewards, milestones and storytelling were common features ([Ho et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12199787/)). reSET, for substance use disorder, got FDA De Novo authorisation in 2017, but its trial tested a desktop version and gave the treatment group up to about $100 in vouchers, which may have driven part of the effect ([PMC12657867](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12657867/); [FDA DEN160018](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/reviews/DEN160018.pdf)). reSET-O for opioid use "failed to show superiority" on abstinence and improved only retention ([PMC12657867](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12657867/)). Their maker, Pear Therapeutics, filed for bankruptcy in April 2023 after insurers would not pay ([pharmaphorum](https://pharmaphorum.com/news/pear-tx-files-bankruptcy-will-seek-sell-assets)). Gaming disorder is itself a recognised diagnosis (ICD-11 code 6C51) ([PMC11203952](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11203952/)), yet **no studies were found on whether streaks or loss-aversion features in wellness apps cause compulsive use**, which is an open gap rather than evidence of safety.

## Neurological conditions and older adults: movement and extra practice do the work

In **stroke**, the 2025 Cochrane review (72 trials, 2,470 people) found VR and gaming improved arm function when **added to** usual care (SMD 0.49) and helped daily activities (SMD 0.25), but was **no better than conventional therapy when it replaced it**, and did not improve walking speed ([Cochrane](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD008349_virtual-reality-stroke-rehabilitation)). The benefit comes from giving patients more therapy time. MindMotion PRO and GO, gamified rehab systems, have FDA 510(k) clearance, which is based on similarity to existing devices rather than proof of superior results ([MindMaze](https://mindmazetherapeutics.com/fda-clears-gamified-neurorehabilitation-system/); [FDA K173931](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/pdf17/K173931.pdf)). In **Parkinson's**, a network meta-analysis of 60 trials ranked exergaming first of all exercise types for a get-up-and-walk test (**4.5 seconds faster** than control) ([Front Aging Neurosci 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/aging-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnagi.2023.1215495/pdf)). In **multiple sclerosis**, computerised cognitive training improved thinking tests by a small amount (g = 0.30), strongest for attention and processing speed (g = 0.32), but daily function and fatigue results were inconclusive and gains faded when training stopped ([Lampit 2019](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1545968319860490)). **Brain injury** reviews exist but their results could not be verified ([PLOS One 2020](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0235510)).

For **memory problems and dementia**, the picture splits. In mild cognitive impairment, Cochrane rates the evidence **very low** and notes that **no trial measured whether people developed dementia** ([Cochrane CD012279](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD012279_computerised-cognitive-training-preventing-dementia-people-mild-cognitive-impairment)). In mild-to-moderate dementia, cognitive training improved thinking-test scores compared with no training (SMD 0.42, moderate quality) but **not compared with other activities** (SMD 0.21, not significant) ([BJPsych Advances](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/bjpsych-advances/article/cognitive-training-for-people-with-mild-to-moderate-dementia-a-cochrane-review/C6F3C93BDC0335844F483EDB98062B3E)). That suggests much of the benefit is from being engaged, not from the specific task. In cognitively healthy older adults, 12 or more weeks of computer training gives a small, low-certainty benefit with no proof it prevents dementia ([Cochrane CD012277](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD012277_computerised-cognitive-training-maintaining-cognitive-function-cognitively-healthy-people-late-life)).

The most striking single result is the **ACTIVE trial's 20-year follow-up** (February 2026). Older adults who did **speed-of-processing training plus booster sessions** were diagnosed with dementia less often (**40% versus 49%** of controls), described as a 25% lower rate. Memory and reasoning training showed no such effect, about 75% of participants had died by follow-up, and it needs replicating ([Johns Hopkins](https://www.hopkinsmedicine.org/news/newsroom/news-releases/2026/02/cognitive-speed-training-linked-to-lower-dementia-incidence-up-to-20-years-later); [journal article](https://alz-journals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/trc2.70197)). The NeuroRacer study found that older adults trained on a driving-and-signs multitasking game improved multitasking beyond untrained 20-year-olds, with gains lasting 6 months, but it was a small lab study with no clinical outcome ([Nature 2013](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature12486)). In July 2026 the WHO recommended "cognitive training and cognitive stimulation and engagement in social activities" for older adults, without singling out apps ([WHO 2026](https://www.who.int/news/item/15-07-2026-new-who-guidelines--up-to-45--of-dementia-risk-could-be-prevented-or-delayed)).

For **healthy ageing**, **movement is the active ingredient**. Exercise games **nearly halved the rate of falls** (IRR 0.53) and cut the number of people who fell by a quarter at 12 months (RR 0.75), with good usability and no serious side effects, though the authors say more trials are needed ([JMIR Aging 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13427072/)). Serious games reduced depression in older adults (SMD −0.54), but only the **physical** games worked; **thinking-only games had no significant effect** ([PMC9490522](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9490522/)). No meta-analysis on loneliness was found, though a scoping review on gaming and loneliness exists ([Healthcare 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/healthcare13172140)). Regulators have acted against overreach here: in 2016 Lumosity paid $2 million to settle FTC charges that it made unsupported claims about delaying cognitive decline, protecting against dementia and helping people with ADHD, stroke and PTSD, and the FTC said it "preyed on consumers' fears" ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2016/01/lumosity-pay-2-million-settle-ftc-deceptive-advertising-charges-its-brain-training-program)). A major review concluded brain training improves the trained tasks, with little evidence for everyday thinking ([NPR on Simons et al. 2016](https://www.npr.org/sections/health-shots/2016/10/03/496120962/brain-game-claims-fail-a-big-scientific-test)).

## Chronic physical disease and pain: games move behaviour, not blood tests

In **adults with diabetes, heart risk or obesity**, gamification reliably gets people walking more, but rarely changes clinical measures. The BE ACTIVE trial in people at high heart risk compared gamification, cash incentives and both; the **combined group did best over 12 months and kept walking more during 6 months of follow-up** ([CHIBE](https://chibe.upenn.edu/publications/effect-of-gamification-financial-incentives-or-both-to-increase-physical-activity-among-patients-at-high-risk-of-cardiovascular-events-the-be-active-randomized-controlled-trial/)); the per-arm step numbers in the notes are **unverified**. In the STEP UP trial, adults with obesity using a wearable with points, levels and a social element walked more, with **competition reported as the most effective and longest-lasting** design (**unverified** figures; [JAMA Intern Med 2019](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/2749761)). A follow-up analysis found people responded more to **losing** points than gaining them ([Games Health J 2021](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1089/g4h.2021.0130)). In type 2 diabetes, a meta-analysis of 3 trials (704 patients) found HbA1c fell by just **0.21%**, called a "short-term positive effect", and gamification was described as an add-on to exercise programmes rather than a stand-alone treatment ([PMC9242065](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9242065/)). The Penn diabetes trial reportedly raised steps without improving HbA1c or weight (**unverified**; [JAMA Netw Open 2021](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2780065)). Pokémon Go's step boost reportedly vanished within about six weeks (**unverified**; [PubMed 27965211](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27965211/)). A large post-heart-attack trial of reminders plus lottery rewards reportedly found no gain in medication adherence (**unverified**; [JAMA Intern Med 2017](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/2633258)). For **cancer survivors**, a 2025 trial of gamification for physical activity in breast and prostate cancer survivors appears positive, but numbers were not retrieved ([JACC CardioOncology](https://www.jacc.org/doi/10.1016/j.jaccao.2025.10.001)). **COPD, kidney disease, HIV and TB** have mainly pilots, single-arm studies or design papers ([JMIR 2025 COPD](https://www.jmir.org/2025/1/e65309); [BMC Public Health TB](https://bmcpublichealth.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12889-023-17561-z)).

In **children with chronic disease**, a meta-analysis found games improved knowledge (g = 0.36) and self-care behaviour (g = 0.31) ([Charlier, JAMIA](https://academic.oup.com/jamia/article/23/1/230/2380055)). Re-Mission, a shooter game for young people with cancer, improved measured antibiotic and chemotherapy adherence but not stress or quality of life ([PubMed 18676516](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18676516/)). A diabetes game improved self-care but **not HbA1c** ([PubMed 9183781](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9183781/)). An inner-city asthma game did not improve asthma outcomes ([PubMed 12665729](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12665729/)). Active video games lowered BMI z-score slightly in overweight children (−0.09), without a significant change in weight ([J Public Health 2023](https://academic.oup.com/jpubhealth/article/45/4/935/7231521)).

For **pain**, two different things work. For **chronic low back pain**, RelieVRx, a VR programme, was FDA-authorised in 2021 after a trial of 188 people against a sham nature video ([PMC12657867](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12657867/)), and at least one insurer covers it ([Fierce Healthcare](https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/health-tech/highmark-first-commercial-payer-cover-appliedvrs-vr-device-chronic-lower-back-pain)). For **children's needle procedures**, a meta-analysis of 18 studies (2,663 children) found game and VR distraction cut pain (SMD −0.71) and fear, but results varied enormously (I² = 92%), so the size of the effect is uncertain ([PMC11417701](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11417701/)). This is an in-the-moment benefit, and most of it comes from VR headsets.

## Special populations: pregnancy, caregivers, hospital, disability and rural India are thinly studied

In **pregnancy**, the strongest signal is VR during labour: pooled results showed large drops in pain and anxiety, but the evidence is **very low certainty** because the trials were small, unblinded and extremely inconsistent (I² of 95–97%) ([Frontiers Med 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/medicine/articles/10.3389/fmed.2026.1838637/full)). VR during routine antenatal heart-rate monitoring lowered mothers' anxiety and blood pressure without changing the baby's heart rate, though safety reporting was absent rather than systematic ([PMC12334377](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12334377/)). Gamified breastfeeding and antenatal education have small positive trials, but their numbers were not extracted ([PMC13170080](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13170080/); [PMC12520305](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12520305/)). A UK smoking-cessation app for pregnant smokers (494 analysed) **did not increase quitting**, and engagement was low ([Transl Behav Med 2019](https://academic.oup.com/tbm/article/9/4/583/5054222)). No game trials were found for gestational diabetes, weight gain in pregnancy or postnatal depression; the evidence there is for ordinary health apps ([PMC11841959](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11841959/); [PMC12810747](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12810747/)). **Menopause** digital therapies studied so far are CBT-style apps, not games ([PubMed 40526898](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40526898/)).

For **dementia caregivers**, one randomised trial compared a gamified platform with a resource platform, but its results could not be read ([PMC12519501](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12519501/)). For **hospital and ICU patients**, VR and exergames look safe and feasible ([Frontiers Med 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/medicine/articles/10.3389/fmed.2025.1686453/full)), but the largest trial (344 ICU survivors in 11 hospitals) found a VR video **did not improve PTSD, anxiety, depression or quality of life at six months** ([Critical Care 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13054-026-06186-4)). For **blind and low-vision** users, games using sound and vibration have been built and shown to work technically, but none has been tested for health outcomes ([VI-Bowling, ACM ASSETS](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1878803.1878836); [Eyes-Free Yoga](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/262220766_Eyes-free_yoga_An_exergame_using_depth_cameras_for_blind_low_vision_exercise)). No health game trials were found for **Deaf or hard-of-hearing** users, although general design guidelines cover hearing needs ([Game Accessibility Guidelines](https://gameaccessibilityguidelines.com/full-list/)). For **low-literacy and rural India**, the closest work is card and phone games for refresher training of ASHA community health workers, which are design pilots, not patient trials ([ACM CHI PLAY 2024](https://doi.org/10.1145/3665463.3678819); [ACM Asian HCI 2022](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3516492.3558788)). A pilot trial of health apps in urban and rural India exists but is not clearly about games ([PMC11733870](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11733870/)). Older players can use well-designed games: exergame trials report usability scores above 80 and 87% retention ([JMIR Aging 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13427072/)), and published guidelines cover motor, vision, hearing and cognitive needs ([Game Accessibility Guidelines](https://gameaccessibilityguidelines.com/full-list/)).

## The claim, not the code, decides whether a game is a medical device

In all three markets, what a product **says it does** decides whether it is regulated. In the **US**, the FDA's revised General Wellness guidance (6 January 2026) lets low-risk products encourage a general state of health, and link healthy habits to reducing the risk or impact of certain chronic diseases where that link is well understood. They may not claim to screen, diagnose, monitor or manage a disease, may not refer to "specific diseases, clinical conditions, or diagnostic thresholds", and may not claim "medical grade" performance ([Covington](https://www.cov.com/en/news-and-insights/insights/2026/01/fda-issues-revised-guidance-on-general-wellness-products); [Troutman Pepper Locke](https://www.troutman.com/insights/fdas-2026-guidance-on-general-wellness-devices-policy-for-low-risk-devices/); [FDA guidance](https://fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/general-wellness-policy-low-risk-devices)). A "talk to a professional" prompt is allowed only if it names no condition ([Troutman Pepper Locke](https://www.troutman.com/insights/fdas-2026-guidance-on-general-wellness-devices-policy-for-low-risk-devices/)). Separately, the FTC can act on unsupported health claims even for products that are not devices, as the Lumosity case shows ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2016/01/lumosity-pay-2-million-settle-ftc-deceptive-advertising-charges-its-brain-training-program)).

In the **EU**, guidance MDCG 2019-11 says "general wellness and lifestyle apps without medical claims" are not medical devices; software that informs diagnosis or treatment decisions is at least Class IIa ([MDCG 2019-11](https://health.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2020-09/md_mdcg_2019_11_guidance_en_0.pdf); [OpenRegulatory](https://openregulatory.com/mdcg/mdcg-2019-11)). In **India**, CDSCO's medical device software guidance, reported finalised on 30 July 2026, explicitly does not apply to "general wellness apps"; medical software is sorted into classes A to D and needs licences ([Emergo by UL](https://www.emergobyul.com/news/india-cdsco-finalizes-guidance-medical-device-software); [CDSCO guidance](https://cdsco.gov.in/opencms/export/sites/CDSCO_WEB/Pdf-documents/Guidance-document-on-Medical-Device-Software-under-MDR-2017.pdf)). India also has a **data** rule that applies even to wellness games: the DPDP Rules 2025, notified 14 November 2025, require **verifiable parental consent before processing data of anyone under 18**, with penalties up to ₹200 crore for child-related violations and full compliance due by about May 2027 ([PIB](https://static.pib.gov.in/WriteReadData/specificdocs/documents/2025/nov/doc20251117695301.pdf); [dpdpa.com Rule 10](https://www.dpdpa.com/dpdparules/rule10.html); [ProtectComply](https://protectcomply.com/blog/dpdp-rules-2025-timeline)). The research notes infer, without having read the full exemption text, that a private consumer game should assume the healthcare and education exemptions do not cover it ([PIB](https://static.pib.gov.in/WriteReadData/specificdocs/documents/2025/nov/doc20251117695301.pdf)). The simplest route for a free game is to **collect no personal data from players at all**.

## Safe wording: talk about play and practice, never about patients

The rule of thumb from the FDA, EU and CDSCO guidance is: describe the **activity** (playing, practising, relaxing), never a **disease** or **clinical result** ([Covington](https://www.cov.com/en/news-and-insights/insights/2026/01/fda-issues-revised-guidance-on-general-wellness-products); [MDCG 2019-11](https://health.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2020-09/md_mdcg_2019_11_guidance_en_0.pdf); [Emergo](https://www.emergobyul.com/news/india-cdsco-finalizes-guidance-medical-device-software)). The evidence gives a second reason: brain-game gains rarely reach daily life, so claims about daily life would also be untrue ([Westwood 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36977764/); [NPR on Simons 2016](https://www.npr.org/sections/health-shots/2016/10/03/496120962/brain-game-claims-fail-a-big-scientific-test)).

| Pocket Plays **can** say | Pocket Plays **must not** say | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "Short games to practise focus skills" | "Treats ADHD" / "An ADHD game" | Naming a disease makes it a device claim ([Troutman](https://www.troutman.com/insights/fdas-2026-guidance-on-general-wellness-devices-policy-for-low-risk-devices/)); EndeavorRx needed a De Novo trial ([FDA DEN200026](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/reviews/DEN200026.pdf)) |
| "Practise remembering sequences" | "Improves memory" / "Boosts working memory for school" | Gains stay on the trained task ([Westwood 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36977764/)) |

| "Fun puzzles to keep your mind busy" / "Enjoy classic board games" | "Keeps your brain young" / "Prevents dementia" / "Slows cognitive decline" | The FTC fined Lumosity for these claims ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2016/01/lumosity-pay-2-million-settle-ftc-deceptive-advertising-charges-its-brain-training-program)) |
| "Something to do while you wait" | "Reduces pain during injections" / "Distraction therapy" | Evidence is mostly VR, with uncertain size ([PMC11417701](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11417701/)) |
| "Play together with grandchildren" | "Reduces loneliness" | Only a scoping review exists, no pooled trial evidence ([Healthcare 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/healthcare13172140)) |
| "Games you can play with one tap" / "Large buttons, no timer" | "Designed for stroke / cerebral palsy rehab" | Rehab claims are device claims; benefits come from movement ([Cochrane](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD008349_virtual-reality-stroke-rehabilitation)) |
| "Not a medical product. If you have concerns about your health, talk to a professional." | "If you score low, you may have ADHD" / "Check your attention" | Screening and naming a condition are device claims ([Troutman](https://www.troutman.com/insights/fdas-2026-guidance-on-general-wellness-devices-policy-for-low-risk-devices/)) |
| "Made for fun and practice, alongside everyday life" | "Recommended by doctors" / "Clinically proven" / "Medical grade" | Unsupported; "medical grade" is excluded from wellness ([Covington](https://www.cov.com/en/news-and-insights/insights/2026/01/fda-issues-revised-guidance-on-general-wellness-products)) |
| "Contains flashing effects" (until fixed) | "Safe for everyone" | Four games can exceed 3 flashes per second ([WCAG 2.3.1](https://w3c.github.io/wcag21/understanding/three-flashes-or-below-threshold.html)) |

One more caution: a clinician or teacher may choose to use Pocket Plays with a patient or pupil, and that is their decision. The app itself should not be marketed "for" a diagnosis, including on social media, app descriptions and landing pages, because regulators look at all marketing when judging intended purpose ([MDCG 2019-11](https://health.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2020-09/md_mdcg_2019_11_guidance_en_0.pdf)).

### Where Pocket Plays is not a fit

Pocket Plays should say plainly that it does not do the things that drive most of the proven results. **Physical-activity and exergame programmes** produce the falls reduction in older adults ([JMIR Aging 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13427072/)), the balance gains in Parkinson's ([Front Aging Neurosci 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/aging-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnagi.2023.1215495/pdf)), stroke arm rehab ([Cochrane](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD008349_virtual-reality-stroke-rehabilitation)), motor gains in cerebral palsy and DCD ([JMIR Serious Games 2022](https://games.jmir.org/2022/4/e38972)), step increases in heart and diabetes patients ([BE ACTIVE](https://chibe.upenn.edu/publications/effect-of-gamification-financial-incentives-or-both-to-increase-physical-activity-among-patients-at-high-risk-of-cardiovascular-events-the-be-active-randomized-controlled-trial/)) and mood gains in older adults ([PMC9490522](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9490522/)); tapping a phone is not exercise. **VR** is behind labour-pain ([Frontiers Med 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/medicine/articles/10.3389/fmed.2026.1838637/full)), chronic back pain ([PMC12657867](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12657867/)) and most needle-pain results ([PMC11417701](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11417701/)). **CBT content** is what makes SPARX, Sleepio, Somryst, DaylightRx, MamaLift Plus and the DiGA mental-health apps work ([PubMed 22773784](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22773784/); [PMC12657867](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12657867/)); Pocket Plays teaches no therapy. **Dichoptic eye treatment** for amblyopia needs special displays ([FDA DEN210005](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/reviews/DEN210005.pdf)). **Social and competitive mechanics** (team leaderboards, partners, cash) drove the Penn step results ([Games Health J 2021](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1089/g4h.2021.0130)) and the autism social-skills results ([Frontiers Pediatr 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/pediatrics/articles/10.3389/fped.2025.1498563/full)); Pocket Plays is single-player and free. **Medication, logging and adherence tools** (Re-Mission, mySugr, reSET) track or reward health behaviours Pocket Plays does not touch ([PubMed 18676516](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18676516/); [mySugr](https://www.mysugr.com/en/blog/story-behind-diabetes-monster-and-point-system)). The **Tetris trauma protocol** is a timed clinical procedure, so Block Fit should never be presented as a trauma aid ([Collabra 2025](https://online.ucpress.edu/collabra/article/11/1/130791/207825/Evidence-That-Tetris-Reduces-Immediate-but-Not)). Pocket Plays is also a poor fit for **blind and low-vision users**, since it is visual; Sound Memory is the only mainly audio game, and proven accessible designs use sound plus vibration ([VI-Tennis](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/234804021_VI-Tennis_A_vibrotactileaudio_exergame_for_players_who_are_visually_impaired)).

### Fix the flashing before promoting to any patient group

Bullseye, Opposite, Rule Switch (red and green) and Clash flash the **whole screen on every tap**, and fast tapping can exceed **three flashes per second**. That breaks the WCAG limit ([WCAG 2.3.1](https://w3c.github.io/wcag21/understanding/three-flashes-or-below-threshold.html)) and sits inside the 3–30 Hz range that triggers photosensitive seizures, with onset most common at ages 7–19 ([Epilepsy Society](https://epilepsysociety.org.uk/about-epilepsy/epileptic-seizures/seizure-triggers/photosensitive-epilepsy)), overlapping the 8–17 age band that EndeavorRx targets for ADHD ([FDA K231337](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/pdf23/K231337.pdf)). WCAG singles out saturated red flashes as a particular risk ([WCAG 2.3.1](https://w3c.github.io/wcag21/understanding/three-flashes-or-below-threshold.html)), which matters for Rule Switch. The fix is to replace full-screen flashes with a small, soft feedback cue (for example, a brief highlight on the tapped item only), and to cap any flash to fewer than three per second ([Game Accessibility Guidelines](https://gameaccessibilityguidelines.com/avoid-flickering-images-and-repetitive-patterns/)). Until then, those four games should not be suggested for children, hospital patients or anyone with epilepsy. Streaks also deserve care: they are the kind of reward mechanic that raises engagement, but no study has checked whether they cause compulsive use in wellness apps ([Ho 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12199787/); [PMC11203952](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11203952/)), so a gentle, forgiving streak is safer than one that punishes a missed day.

## Conclusion

The research points to a clear split. Where games help patients in measurable, real-world ways, they do so by **delivering something else**: movement, eye training, therapy content, cash-backed accountability or immersive distraction. The game makes people do more of it. Where games are the whole intervention, as with brain training, they reliably improve the game-like task and not much else, and even the one regulator-cleared example, EndeavorRx, is labelled only for a computer test score ([FDA K231337](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/pdf23/K231337.pdf)). For Pocket Plays, that means the honest pitch is also the legally safe one: a pleasant, accessible way to practise focus, memory and calm, which some families, older adults and patients may enjoy alongside their real care. The most valuable next steps are practical, not scientific: fix the four flashing games, keep all marketing disease-free, avoid collecting personal data (especially from under-18 users in India), and add accessibility options such as sound-off, larger targets and no-timer modes.

The larger lesson is that "which patients can games help?" is the wrong question for a free wellness app. The better question is "which patients can **use and enjoy** Pocket Plays safely?" That question has a broad answer, including older adults, children with ADHD or autism, people with limited hand control, carers and hospital patients, as long as the app never claims to treat any of them. If Pocket Plays ever wants a medical claim, the path is the one EndeavorRx and the DiGA apps took, a controlled trial with a specific, measurable outcome, and the DiGA delistings show that even that path often fails ([npj Digital Medicine 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-025-01879-6)).
